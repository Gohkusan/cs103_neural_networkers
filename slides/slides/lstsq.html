Recall · Week 11 · eigenvalues

A one-layer linear net is least squares, and η is set by eigenvalues

Samples as rows: A = Xᵀ (m × n), weights w, targets y, φ = identity, no bias

ℓ = ½‖Aw − y‖² ⟹ gradient Aᵀ(Aw − y)

gradient = 0 ⟹ AᵀAŵ = Aᵀy normal equations

error eₖ = wₖ − ŵ: eₖ₊₁ = [I − ηAᵀA]eₖ

converges ⟺ 0 < η < 2/λₘₐₓ(AᵀA)

Why the bound

A difference equation: each eigen-direction of AᵀA shrinks by 1 − ηλᵢ per step, so every |1 − ηλᵢ| must be below 1.

We checked it

η = 0.9(2/λₘₐₓ) converged; η = 1.05(2/λₘₐₓ) blew up. Once φ bends, no normal equations exist: only descent.

Here y plays the Week 10 right-hand side b; we renamed it to keep b for biases. Sources: Goodfellow et al. Ch. 4; Goh (2017).

[Haide, about 50 s] Neural networks generalise something we already trust. Take one layer, no activation, squared loss, and put the samples as the rows of A. The loss is half the squared norm of A w minus y. Its gradient is A-transpose times the residual; setting it to zero gives exactly the Week 10 normal equations. Note we renamed the book's b to y, because b is our bias. Gradient descent instead takes small steps. Subtract the solution and the error obeys e_(k+1) = (I minus eta A-transpose A) e_k, a Week 11 difference equation. A-transpose A is symmetric with non-negative eigenvalues, so in its eigenbasis each direction is multiplied by 1 minus eta lambda_i every step. That shrinks only if eta is between 0 and 2 over the largest eigenvalue. We checked: 0.9 of the bound converged, 1.05 of it diverged. Why not just solve the normal equations? For big problems it is costly and badly conditioned, and once phi is non-linear there is no closed form at all, so descent is the only general tool.
